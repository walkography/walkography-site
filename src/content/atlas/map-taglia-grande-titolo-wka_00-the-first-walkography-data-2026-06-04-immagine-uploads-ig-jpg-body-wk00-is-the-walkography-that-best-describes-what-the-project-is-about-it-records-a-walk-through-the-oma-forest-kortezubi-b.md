WK00 is the walkography that best describes what the project is about. It records a walk through the Oma forest (Kortezubi, Bizkaia) on 26/03/2016.

It is a geolocated map at a precise geometric scale, and at first glance the path dominates. The choice is intentional — it gives the drawing geometric rigour and makes it a "map" in the most conventional sense. But that conventional rigour gradually comes apart. The surrounding paths blur into the background pattern, the planimetric layer loses weight, and it becomes clear that the rendering of the route is reaching for something else. The intention to communicate the intangible becomes explicit.

The background pattern is a sequence of eucalyptus trunks — an invasive species common in the forests of Atlantic Spain. The Oma forest is known as an open-air art installation, thanks to the paintings made on the trunks of its trees. In the map, the tree is treated as a work of art in itself. There is no trace of the paintings: they were already familiar, in some sense "discovered" before the walk ever began. This places WK00 at once in a specific site — through the coordinates and the precise route — and in every forest in the world. It represents a walk among eucalyptuses in which place, walker, and the conditions of the moment all sit on the same semantic plane.

The trunks make the primacy of eye-level vision explicit. They are read most clearly at the start, during the climb: the trunks are larger and the rhythm more measured, because the walker is less tired and more drawn to the novelty of the landscape. Then the descent. Pace and speed pick up, and with them the rhythm of the pattern. In parallel, transparency introduces another rhythm — a falling-off of interest, a less precise grasp of the surroundings, driven by fatigue and by the fading of novelty.

All of this points to a single requirement: each exploration must be approached as a conscious act, almost as a kind of work. The map was drawn once the walk was over. Few photographs were taken, no notes were kept, and as much room as possible was left to perception itself. The point was to make space for the unforeseen — including in its unconscious dimension — so that it could be caught and accepted. This is what keeps the experience from being a purely subjective operation — one might even say a selfish, self-referential one — and turns it into a way of exploring the relationship between subject and surroundings.

In this respect, Andrea Zanzotto's words feel particularly close:
[il paesaggio e l'orizzonte, a 360 gradi] *In cui noi siamo una specie di centro mobile, […] qualcosa che punge e trapunge e di cui noi siamo una specie di spoletta che si aggira in mezzo… che cuce… oppure di qualcosa che taglia (*).*

Retracing the route afterwards from memory made it possible to sediment, filter, and prioritise the most relevant aspects, leaving in the map only what felt fundamental.
Any map — even a conventional topographic one — is an abstraction of the territory it claims to represent. Walkography starts from that assumption. WK00 places geometric abstraction and perceptual abstraction on the same plane. The result is unique and unrepeatable — shaped by the subject and by the moment — and makes no claim to be absolute or unquestionable. It is, first and foremost, a narrative.

Most of the principles Walkography rests on today are already there, in nascent form: the primacy of first-person perception, the difficulty of representing the subjective, the introduction of fatigue, attention and emotion as legitimate data.

In the ten years that followed, walkographies were very few — I'll address the reasons more fully in another post. The main difficulty was narrating and recording the intangible in a way credible enough to bring it up to the status of "scientific data". The subjectivity–objectivity argument set out in the theoretical texts on Walkography ([link]) proved harder to put on the page than expected. This new chapter of Walkography exists, in part, to take that problem up again.
